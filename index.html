What you are looking at

SPHEREx is a NASA telescope that photographs the whole sky every six months, in 102 colours of infrared light. This page takes one small patch of that sky and shows it pass after pass, like flipping between two photographs to find what changed.

Every image is put on the same grid, with north up and east to the left. Stars far away stay still. A star close to us drifts across the background, and the blink makes that drift jump out. Astronomers have found nearby stars and brown dwarfs this way for more than a century, first with glass plates and since 2018 with WiseView, the tool this page is modelled on.

The difference view subtracts the average of all images from each one. What never changes cancels out and turns grey. Something that moved leaves a white spot where it is now and a dark spot where it used to be.

How to use it

  1. Pick one of the five targets under the image, or open Settings and type any position.
  2. Watch the blink, or press space to stop and step with ← and →.
  3. Switch between one image per sky pass and every single exposure. A pass is the median of all exposures from the same six months, which is cleaner. Single exposures show the exact date of each picture.
  4. Press d for the difference view and m to hide the marker.
  5. Copy the link to send someone exactly what you see.

How the images are made

When you ask for a field, the server asks the NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive for every SPHEREx exposure that covers it. For each one it moves the target to the date of that exposure using its proper motion, downloads a small cutout around it, and subtracts the glow of dust in our own solar system that SPHEREx models in every file. It then resamples every cutout onto one fixed grid of 6.15 arcsec pixels, so the same pixel always means the same spot on the sky.

SPHEREx has six detectors, each behind a filter whose colour changes from one edge to the other. A star therefore falls at a slightly different wavelength in every exposure, and the label on the image says which. That is also why each image gets its own brightness stretch.

This is the weekend version. It shows one detector at a time, a bright star like Barnard's saturates in the middle, and a new field has to wait for the archive. The data and the code are all public, so every image here can be checked.
